About

Where the work comes from

I'm a health services researcher and study coordinator based in Minneapolis, working at the intersection of clinical care, data, and the people navigating both. At the VA, that has meant helping build a natural language model that reads provider notes for signs a veteran may have access to a firearm, and it has meant sitting on the phone with a veteran while she talks through the fear of an abnormal cancer screening result. Both are part of the same job: making sure the systems meant to protect people actually notice them in time.

Before health services research, I studied psychology at Vanderbilt University. In between, I spent a year as a full-time caregiver at a residential foster care facility in Houston, and almost three years as an overnight house manager at the Ronald McDonald House in Nashville. Those jobs taught me as much about steadying people through hard moments as any methods course did, and that shows up in how I run studies now: carefully, and with the person on the other end of the data always in view.

Research

Publications

2025 JAMIA

Comparison of six natural language processing approaches to assessing firearm access in Veterans Health Administration electronic health records

Trujeque J, Dudley RA, Mesfin N, Ingraham NE, Ortiz I, Bangerter A, Chakraborty A, Schutte D, Yeung J, Liu Y, Woodward‑Abel A, Bromley E, Zhang R, Brenner LA, Simonetti JA.

This study compared six models for classifying whether a veteran's clinical notes describe having access to a firearm, no access, or an unclear situation. Four traditional machine learning models were tested against two clinical language models built on BERT. The best performer, Bio‑ClinicalBERT, reached an F1 score of 0.896, well above the older random forest approach used in prior work. Firearm access is a strong, independent risk factor for suicide, and there's no diagnosis code for it, so text like this is often the only record of it that exists. I supported this project's IRB documentation, data management, and manuscript preparation.

Read the paper → doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocae169
2026 Patient Education and Counseling

Co-production of a conversation guide for HPV-based cervical cancer screening results communication

Danan ER, Woodward‑Abel AB, Gowdy‑Jaehnig A, Friedman JK, Hutchison CA, Bromley EF, Krebs EE, Spoont M, Matthias MS.

As HPV testing replaces the Pap smear as the standard for cervical cancer screening, patients are left needing a very different conversation with their clinician. We interviewed 21 women veterans about how abnormal results should be explained to them, then built a conversation guide around what they told us: say plainly that a result is not cancer, give a clear roadmap for what happens next, and don't frame HPV the way clinicians frame other sexually transmitted infections, since that framing tends to add shame without adding clarity. This was my project as study coordinator, from interview scheduling through qualitative analysis and drafting the guide itself.

Beyond Research

The rest of it

I co-founded and led Double Major, an a cappella group at Vanderbilt, from the ground up: by-laws, auditions, budgets, and four years of shows. More recently I co-led the CCDOR Inclusion Workgroup at the Minneapolis VA, where we ran a buddy system for new hires and organized community events across a research center that can otherwise feel siloed by project.

Earlier still, I spent teenage summers doing direct-service volunteer work in Pennsylvania: running a program for tween girls in unsafe neighborhoods at the Delaware County Family Center, and bringing music therapy into an early intervention preschool classroom through Merakey. Different setting, same instinct as the work I do now.
